This was supposed to be a park

In 1940 the city opened this road as part of a park.1 Parkways then were for “pleasure vehicles.”2 Today it is a highway, and the park lives beside it.

History

The park the city promised

The city made this shore for a park. By 1940 it had built a sea wall out in the Narrows and pumped sand behind it, “thus manufacturing new land for recreation purposes.”8 The Belt Parkway opened along it on 29 June 1940, as part of that park.1

“A continuous park system, not merely an automobile artery, a system of shoestring parks and recreational facilities encircling the entire metropolis”Robert Moses, Parks Commissioner, as quoted on a Parks sign in Shore Road Park.9

Bay Ridge was promised a waterfront park like Riverside Park in Manhattan.8

Today

Bay Ridge, cut off from its bay

This is the Bay Ridge waterfront, two miles from the ferry pier to the bridge.10 Most of it is Shore Road Park.11 Zoning marks the rest, the road, as park too.12

About 16.5 acres of it is highway.4 The road cuts the park in two. Playgrounds and ball fields sit on one side.13,11 On the other, the strip left by the water is about 70 to 80 feet wide at most sections.7

About 55,700 vehicles a day use this part of the Belt.14 Since 2021, crashes here have killed 3 people.5

About 35,300 people live within half a mile.6 To reach the water, they climb 1940 footbridges8,15 or walk up to a mile to a crossing without steps.7

Park

The same land, with the road taken out

One park instead of two halves would put the playgrounds, the lawns and the water on the same side. People already fish from the rail, walk and cycle here.16 Without the fence, the guardrail and the road, they could reach the water without crossing a highway or climbing a footbridge,15 and the shore would be quieter.

In Section

Taking the road out wouldn’t level the climb. Shore Road sits 18 to 73 feet above sea level, and at six of the eight sections measured the slope down is steeper than a ramp may be.18,15 Without a road in the way, a park would have room for gentler paths up the slope.

New York already runs parks this way. Prospect Park and Central Park drives are closed to private cars and open to park and emergency vehicles.21,22 A park here would need what every park needs, someone to run it and a budget to keep it up.

Evidence

What the records show

Origins

The fill made room for the parkway and about 23 acres of new park between it and Shore Road.23,24 Much of the strip is fill, but not all of it. A 2005 report for NYC DOT reads the 92nd Street site as “at least in part an existing shore,” raised with debris and boulders.25

The opening announcement called the parkway “a narrow shoestring park.”1 The ribbon was cut in Bay Ridge, opposite Owl’s Head Park just north of 68th Street. The whole Belt Parkway cost $28 million to build. The city paid $16 million and a federal Public Works Administration grant paid $12 million.1 By 1942 the strip between Shore Road and the parkway held five playgrounds, two athletic fields, bicycle paths and promenades.13

Shore

The road takes about 75 to 90 feet of the 320 to 680 feet between the water and Shore Road.4,7 On the Parks property map, the land on both sides is one park. Parks calls it Shore Park and Parkway, and its own signs call it Shore Road Park. Parks lists it at 58 acres, with ball fields, courts, playgrounds, a dog run and the Narrows Botanical Gardens.26,11,9

Crossings

To reach the water, people have to cross the road. There are four ways across, at the same places as in 1942.13,7

The two in the middle have stairs. A 2024 City Comptroller audit found two footbridges in this park with steps that don’t meet current ADA standards, most likely these two. It also listed corroded railings at both and holes in the girders of one.15,8

The step-free crossings sit at the two ends, about two miles apart. Someone at 86th Street who can’t use stairs walks about a mile to reach either one.7,27 Within half a mile of the road, about 14,600 people live more than half a mile from a step-free way across.6

Traffic

Commercial vehicles are barred, with some exceptions by permit.28,29 In 1942 the elevated Gowanus link was also a parkway, “limited to passenger vehicles.”30 It later became part of I-278, now one of the city’s through truck routes.31 So the region already has its expressway, and this road remains from an earlier idea of what a parkway was for.

NYSDOT’s 55,700 a day comes from a 2018 count adjusted for growth.14,32 The Gowanus Expressway, half a mile to a mile inland, carries about 109,700.7,14 The road is busy, so the case rests on what the land is worth, not on empty lanes.

Just north of the pier, at Exit 1, a crash on 25 August 2026 killed three teenagers.33

Land

The tax map puts the whole strip from the water to Shore Road, road included, in one lot listed under the Parks Department.34 Zoning marks it as park.12 The City Map labels it Belt Parkway, a mapped street more than 300 feet wide.35 The Parks property map leaves the road out, and NYC DOT does road work on the Belt.26,36 None of these is a title record. No private lot appears between the water and Shore Road at any section, so buying private land doesn’t look necessary there. Changes to the City Map go through the city’s public land use review.37

Cost

What the park would take

The main cost is one link. Without the waterfront stretch, trips between the Belt and the Gowanus would use local streets near 4th Avenue, a longer route east through Queens, or new ramps joining the Belt to I-278 at the Verrazzano.7 Nobody has counted how many drivers make this movement.

No direct ramp links the bridge and the waterfront. Signs send some Staten Island trips between them by way of 4th Avenue and 92nd Street.7 The ramp onto the parkway at 4th Avenue heading north, the northbound exit to 65th and 67th Streets (Exit 1) and the southbound ramp from Ridge Boulevard would go.7

Verrazzano

The MTA is rebuilding the ramps between the bridge and the Belt under project VN-84B. Its November 2025 schedule planned ramp work to start in spring 2026 and the project to finish in late 2029.38 The proposal keeps the bridge approaches. Drivers heading west would leave the Belt at Exit 2, at 4th Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway, at the latest.7

Ferry Pier

The park ends at American Veterans Memorial Pier, the Bay Ridge ferry landing at 69th Street.39 The pier is reached from Bay Ridge Avenue, under the parkway. North of the pier the Belt runs about 0.4 miles to the Gowanus, and those 0.4 miles could stay road or end as a short road to the pier.7

Questions

What people ask

Where will the traffic go?

Some would move to the Gowanus and other roads, and some trips would change or stop. Where roads have been cut in other cities, less than half of the lost traffic showed up on other roads on average. That is a pattern from elsewhere, not a forecast for this road.40 When the city closed the Central Park and Prospect Park drives in stages in 2015, the mayor said it had been “pleasantly surprised that there haven’t been traffic impacts.”41

Won’t drivers just cut through the neighborhood?

Some would, mostly near the ramps at the two ends. Shore Road and nearby streets would need changes so the Gowanus stays the faster way.

Could we try it first?

Yes. Close the stretch to cars for a month in summer, and count traffic on the Gowanus and local streets before, during and after. Paris started its riverside park this way, closing an expressway for a month each summer from 2002 before closing it for good.42

I drive in from Long Island. What happens to me?

If you’re going to Staten Island, nothing. That trip doesn’t use this stretch, and the bridge approaches stay.7 If you’re heading for the Gowanus, you’d go through Queens or by local streets near 4th Avenue.7

What about people who have to drive to work?

Nobody loses their car. Drivers lose one two-mile link, and the Gowanus Expressway runs alongside it, half a mile to a mile inland.7,14 In Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights, nearly twice as many workers take transit as drive alone.43

How would ambulances get through?

The same way they do in Central Park and Prospect Park, where the drives stay open to emergency vehicles.21,22

Why this, when the park we have needs repairs?

The repairs come first, and they shouldn’t wait for this. Parks is already planning to rebuild the promenade, with more than $10 million in funding.44 While that work is underway, it’s a good time to ask what the whole shore should be.

Who would pay for it?

Nobody has priced it yet. Parks already runs the park on both sides of the road.11

Won’t a park attract traffic?

Not as much as a highway.

Would we build a highway here today?

If the answer is no, the next question is what this shore should be. This page is here to start that conversation.

Resources

Where cities have done this

In New York

  • Hudson River Park, Manhattan. The elevated West Side Highway collapsed in 1973, and the interstate planned to replace it was dropped in 1985. In 1998 the state made the shore a park.
  • Sheridan Boulevard, the Bronx. In 2019 the state turned about 1.3 miles of the Sheridan Expressway into a boulevard, with new crosswalks to Starlight and Concrete Plant parks.
  • Brooklyn Waterfront Greenway. A 29-mile route from Greenpoint to East New York, 22 miles of it built. It is planned to join the Shore Parkway Greenway in Bay Ridge.

Elsewhere

  • Harbor Drive, Portland. A six-lane waterfront highway, opened in 1950 and closed in 1974. Its land became a 37-acre waterfront park, now Tom McCall Waterfront Park.
  • Embarcadero Freeway, San Francisco. Damaged in the 1989 earthquake and taken down by 1991. A boulevard with a streetcar line and a waterfront promenade replaced it.
  • Cheonggye Freeway, Seoul. An elevated highway from 1968, taken down from 2003 to 2005 and replaced by a creek and a 9-kilometer green strip.
  • Madrid Río, Madrid. Part of the M-30 ring road along the Manzanares went underground, and a 10-kilometer park opened in its place in 2011.
  • Rives de Seine, Paris. A riverside expressway closed each summer from 2002, until the city approved closing it for good in 2012.

Under way and proposed

  • Interstate 81, Syracuse. New York State is taking down the elevated highway through the city center.
  • Freeways Without Futures. A list of city highways that would be better removed, published every two years since 2008 by the Congress for the New Urbanism.
